# The House Beyond the Rain ## Chapter One — The Letter The rain began before sunrise and continued without pause, turning the quiet streets into narrow rivers that reflected the pale yellow lights of the houses. Aarav stood beside his bedroom window and watched the drops slide slowly down the glass. He had been awake for nearly an hour, although he had no particular reason to be. Sleep had left him suddenly, as if someone had called his name from another room. On the desk behind him lay an unfinished letter, a cup of cold tea, and an old photograph that he had found three days earlier inside a drawer he rarely opened. The photograph showed his parents standing in front of a house surrounded by tall trees. His mother was smiling at the camera, while his father looked serious, almost uncomfortable, as though he had been asked to stand there against his will. Aarav had seen the photograph many times as a child, but only now had he noticed the house in the background. It was not their family home. He knew every corner of the house where he had grown up, every crack in the kitchen wall, every window that refused to close properly during the monsoon. This house was different. It looked older, larger, and strangely isolated. Behind his parents stood a wooden door with a small silver lock. For some reason, Aarav could not stop thinking about that door. He picked up the letter from his desk and read the first few lines again. He had written to his father three times during the past week and destroyed each version before finishing it. He did not know how to begin a conversation that had been silent for so long. Five years had passed since he had last visited his father's home. Their final conversation had ended badly, although neither of them had shouted. That was what made it worse. There had been no dramatic argument, no broken glass or slammed doors, only a quiet exchange of words that had left both of them wounded. His father had told him that people could spend their entire lives avoiding the truth because they were afraid of what it might cost them. Aarav had replied that sometimes the truth was simply not worth knowing. His father had looked at him for several seconds and said, “One day you'll change your mind.” Aarav had walked out before his father could say anything else. Now, five years later, those words seemed to have followed him through every city, every job, every relationship, and every quiet evening when he found himself remembering the past. His mother had died when he was nineteen, and after her death the house had become unbearably quiet. His father had never been the same. Aarav had tried to understand him, but grief had created a wall between them that neither knew how to climb. Eventually Aarav left home for university and stayed away. He told himself that distance would make everything easier. Instead, distance had only made the silence larger. A knock came at his apartment door. Aarav looked toward the hallway. It was still early, and he was not expecting anyone. The knock came again, softer this time. He walked to the door and opened it. An elderly woman stood outside holding a small brown suitcase. For several seconds neither of them spoke. Then the woman smiled. “You're Aarav,” she said. He frowned. “Yes.” “I knew you would look like her.” “Like who?” “Your mother.” The answer unsettled him. He studied the woman's face, trying to remember where he had seen her before. There was something familiar about her voice, something that belonged to a distant part of his childhood. “My name is Meera Sen,” she said. “I knew your parents.” Aarav stepped aside and allowed her into the apartment. She walked slowly toward the living room and looked around. Her eyes moved over the bookshelves, the small table near the window, and the framed photograph of Aarav's mother that stood beside a plant. “You kept this,” she said. “Of course.” “She would have been happy.” Aarav did not know what to say. Mrs. Sen placed her suitcase on the floor and opened it. From inside, she removed a wooden box wrapped in a faded piece of cloth. The box was small enough to fit in both hands. Its surface was covered with scratches, and on the top were two initials carved into the wood. Aarav recognized them immediately. His mother's initials. “What is that?” he asked. “Something your mother asked me to keep.” “For me?” Mrs. Sen nodded. “Why didn't you give it to me earlier?” “Because she told me not to.” Aarav stared at her. “She said you would need to be older before you understood what was inside.” He reached for the box, but stopped before touching it. “What is inside?” Mrs. Sen looked at him with a sadness that made him suddenly afraid. “I don't know,” she said. “She never allowed me to open it.” Aarav finally took the box. It was heavier than it looked. On the underside, there was a tiny metal plate with an address engraved on it. He read the address twice. His heart began to beat faster. It was the address of the house in the photograph. The house he had never known. The house his parents had never mentioned. The house that, according to the address, still existed. Mrs. Sen watched his expression carefully. “You should go there,” she said. Aarav looked up. “Why?” “Because your mother wanted you to.” The rain continued outside. And for the first time in five years, Aarav felt that the past was not behind him. It was waiting. ## Chapter Two — The Journey The next morning, Aarav boarded the first train leaving the city. He carried only a small backpack, the wooden box, and the photograph. He had told no one where he was going. Even his closest friend knew only that Aarav had suddenly taken a few days off work. The train moved slowly through the wet countryside, passing fields covered with mist, small villages waking beneath grey skies, and railway crossings where people stood patiently waiting for the carriages to pass. Aarav sat beside the window and kept the wooden box on his lap. Every few minutes he looked down at it as though expecting it to change. He had considered opening it on the train, but something stopped him. Perhaps it was fear. Perhaps it was respect for his mother's final request. Whatever the reason, the box remained closed. His mother's face appeared in his memory throughout the journey. She had always been the calmer person in the family. Where his father was serious and practical, she had been curious and warm. She believed that every person carried invisible stories, and that most arguments happened because people judged the visible part without understanding what lay beneath it. When Aarav was a child, she used to tell him that houses remembered people. He had laughed at her then. He believed houses were simply walls, doors, floors, and windows. She had told him that one day he would understand. He wondered if this was what she had meant. The train reached the small station shortly after noon. The rain had stopped, but the air remained heavy with moisture. Aarav stepped onto the platform and immediately noticed how quiet the place was. There were no crowds, no taxis waiting outside, and no shops filled with impatient passengers. A faded sign announced the name of the town. He had never heard it spoken before. A man driving an old blue car was waiting near the station entrance. “You're Mr. Aarav?” he asked. “Yes.” “I was told to pick you up.” “By whom?” The man smiled. “Your father.” Aarav froze. “My father knows I'm coming?” The driver opened the passenger door. “He has known for several days.” Aarav climbed into the car. The road leaving the station wound through a landscape of green hills and scattered houses. The further they travelled, the fewer people they saw. Eventually the road became narrow and covered with fallen leaves. Tall trees grew on both sides, their branches forming a roof overhead. “How far is it?” Aarav asked. “Twenty minutes.” The driver said nothing more. Aarav looked out of the window. The landscape seemed strangely familiar. Then he saw it. A stone wall covered in moss. A broken iron gate. A long driveway disappearing between trees. His breath caught. The driver slowed down. “We're here.” At the end of the driveway stood the house from the photograph. It was larger than he had imagined. The roof was covered with dark tiles, some broken and some hidden beneath vines. Tall windows looked out over the garden. The walls were pale and weathered, but the building itself remained strong. It seemed less abandoned than asleep. Aarav stepped out of the car. For several seconds he simply stood there. The front door opened. His father appeared. He looked older than Aarav remembered. His hair had turned almost completely white, and he walked with a slight stiffness that had not been there five years earlier. Neither man moved. Then his father said, quietly, “You came.” Aarav swallowed. “You knew?” “Yes.” “How?” “Because I asked Mrs. Sen to find you.” The words left Aarav speechless. His father looked at the wooden box in his hands. “She gave it to you.” Aarav nodded. “Then it's time.” “For what?” His father stepped aside. “To tell you everything.” ## Chapter Three — The Room Upstairs The house smelled of wood, dust, and rain. Aarav followed his father through a long hallway filled with old photographs. He recognized himself in several of them as a child, but there were others he had never seen. One photograph showed his mother standing beside a woman he did not recognize. Another showed his father in front of the same house when he was much younger. The strangest photograph showed three people standing beside a black car. His father was one of them. The other two were strangers. “Who are they?” Aarav asked. His father stopped walking. “Your mother's brother.” Aarav stared at him. “My mother had a brother?” “Yes.” “Why didn't I know?” “Because he disappeared before you were born.” Aarav felt a chill despite the warmth of the hallway. “Disappeared?” His father continued walking. “There are things your mother never wanted you to know.” “And you agreed?” “I did.” “Why?” “Because I promised her.” They reached a staircase. His father climbed slowly. Aarav followed. At the top was a narrow corridor with three doors. His father opened the first. Inside was a small room filled with boxes, books, old furniture, and several framed photographs. Dust covered nearly everything. “This belonged to your mother.” Aarav stepped inside. His eyes immediately went to a writing desk near the window. He recognized it. His mother had owned that desk when he was a child. He walked toward it and touched the surface. “She wrote here,” his father said. Aarav opened the drawer. Inside was a notebook. The cover was blue. He knew it immediately. His mother had used that notebook when he was a child to write down recipes, grocery lists, and small notes. He remembered drawing pictures on its last pages. He opened it. The first few pages were ordinary. Then the writing changed. There were dates. Names. Addresses. And finally, a page with his own name. Aarav read the sentence written beneath it. “Someday Aarav will ask why we kept the house hidden from him.” His hands became cold. He turned the page. “By then, he will be old enough to understand that protecting someone and hiding the truth from them are not always the same thing.” Aarav looked at his father. “What is this place?” His father stood silently near the door. “It was your mother's family home.” “Then why didn't we live here?” “Because after your uncle disappeared, your mother couldn't bear to return.” “What happened to him?” His father looked toward the window. “We don't know.” Aarav closed the notebook. “You said you were going to tell me everything.” “I will.” “Then start.” His father sat down. For a long moment he said nothing. Finally, he spoke. “Your uncle disappeared twenty-eight years ago. He left this house one evening and never returned. There was no body, no message, and no evidence of where he went. Your grandfather searched for him for years. Your mother did too.” “And then?” “Then she stopped searching.” “Why?” “Because she found something.” His father reached into his coat pocket. He removed a small envelope. Aarav recognized the handwriting. His mother's. “She found this in the attic,” his father said. Aarav took the envelope. His name was written on it. Again. He opened it carefully. Inside was a single sheet of paper. There were only a few lines. “Your uncle did not leave because he wanted to disappear. He left because someone wanted him gone.” Aarav read the sentence twice. His father closed his eyes. “There is more,” he said. Aarav looked up. “What?” “The person who wanted him gone was someone we knew.” The room became completely silent. Outside, the rain began again. ## Chapter Four — The Key That night Aarav could not sleep. He lay in the old bedroom that had once belonged to him when he was a child, although he had no memory of ever sleeping there. The room contained a narrow bed, a wooden cupboard, and a window overlooking the garden. On the wall hung a faded painting of a mountain road disappearing into fog. He kept thinking about the sentence in his mother's letter. Someone had wanted his uncle gone. Someone they knew. The possibility disturbed him more than the disappearance itself. Families could survive secrets, but they rarely survived betrayal. At midnight, Aarav heard footsteps in the hallway. He opened the door. His father was standing outside. “Come with me,” he said. They walked downstairs without turning on the lights. The house was silent. His father stopped beside the staircase and pointed toward a small door beneath it. Aarav had never noticed it before. He unlocked the door. Behind it was a narrow staircase leading downward. They descended into darkness. At the bottom was a basement. The air was cold. His father turned on a small lamp. A wooden table stood in the center of the room. On it was a metal box. Aarav stared at it. His father said nothing. Aarav approached. There was a keyhole on the front. He looked down at the wooden box he had been carrying since leaving the city. Then he understood. He opened his box. Inside, beneath the photograph and envelope, was the silver key. His mother had left it for this moment. Aarav inserted it into the metal box. The lock turned. Inside was a collection of letters. Dozens of them. Some were addressed to his mother. Some to his father. Others had no names. Aarav picked up the first letter. The handwriting was unfamiliar. He began reading. The letter described a meeting that had taken place many years ago. It mentioned his uncle, his grandfather, and another man whose name Aarav recognized from the photograph upstairs. He looked at his father. “Who is Daniel?” His father went pale. “You shouldn't have found that name yet.” “Who is he?” His father sat heavily in the chair beside the table. “He was your grandfather's business partner.” “And what did he do?” “He stole money.” Aarav frowned. “That doesn't explain the disappearance.” “No.” His father looked at the letters. “Because Daniel wasn't the only person involved.” Aarav felt his stomach tighten. “Who else?” His father looked directly at him. “Your grandfather.” The lamp flickered. Aarav stepped back. “My grandfather?” “He knew what happened.” “And my mother knew?” “Yes.” “And you?” “Yes.” Aarav stared at his father. “You all knew.” “We knew pieces.” “You let me believe my uncle simply disappeared.” “We were trying to protect you.” “From what?” His father stood. “From the same people who were watching this house.” Aarav looked around the basement. “Are they still watching?” His father did not answer. A sound came from upstairs. A door closing. Both men froze. His father turned off the lamp. They stood in complete darkness. Another sound followed. Footsteps. Someone was walking through the house. Aarav's heart began pounding. His father whispered one sentence. “Do not make a sound.” The footsteps stopped directly above them. Then came three slow knocks on the basement door. Aarav held his breath. A voice came from the other side. “Aarav?” He recognized it. It was Mrs. Sen. His father looked confused. The voice came again. “Aarav, open the door.” But this time something was different. The voice sounded frightened. Very frightened. His father reached for the handle. Then Mrs. Sen whispered through the door. “Your father is not the person you should be afraid of.” Silence followed. Aarav looked at his father. His father looked back at him. Neither spoke. Then the basement lights suddenly came on. The door was still locked. But the footsteps upstairs had disappeared. On the table, beside the metal box, lay a new envelope. It had not been there before. Aarav slowly picked it up. His name was written across the front. And beneath it were four words. “Read this before morning.” He opened it. The first line made his blood run cold. “Your uncle is alive.” ## Chapter Five — The Road Through the Forest By sunrise, the house was empty. Mrs. Sen had disappeared. His father refused to explain where she had gone. The envelope containing the message was now lying open on the kitchen table, beside a map that showed a remote mountain village several hours away. A red circle had been drawn around a small building near the edge of the forest. “He is there,” his father said. Aarav looked at the map. “You believe this?” “I have no choice.” “You said he was missing.” “I said we didn't know where he was.” “That's not the same thing.” His father looked exhausted. “No.” Aarav folded the map. “Then we're going.” They left the house shortly after seven. The road climbed steadily through the mountains. The forest grew thicker as they travelled, and the villages became smaller. For several hours neither father nor son spoke. The silence between them was different now. It was no longer the silence of anger. It was the silence of two people who had discovered that their memories of the same family were completely different. Eventually Aarav broke the silence. “Why didn't you tell me?” His father kept his eyes on the road. “Because your mother asked me not to.” “Why?” “Because she believed that if you knew the truth, you would search for your uncle.” “And?” “She was afraid you would find the people who had tried to silence him.” Aarav looked out the window. “You should have trusted me.” His father said nothing. The car continued through the forest. After another hour they reached the village. There was only one road. At the end stood a small house with a blue door. Aarav stepped out. An old man was waiting outside. He looked at Aarav. Then at his father. Then he smiled. “I wondered when you would come.” Aarav felt his heart stop for a moment. “Who are you?” The man stepped aside. “Come inside.” Aarav entered. On the wall was a photograph. The man in the photograph was younger. But Aarav recognized him immediately. It was his uncle. His father's brother. The man standing beside the blue door was not a stranger. He was the person Aarav had spent his entire childhood believing was dead. His uncle looked at him for a long time. Then he said quietly, “You have your mother's eyes.” Aarav could not speak. His father closed the door behind them. Outside, the forest moved gently in the wind. Inside the small house, twenty-eight years of silence were finally beginning to end.
